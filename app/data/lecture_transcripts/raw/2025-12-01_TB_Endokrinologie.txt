So the kidney typically gets one artery—albeit a very big one—but it gets one artery, whereas the adrenal glands, these small glands sitting here, each one gets three arteries typically. Extremely high blood supply relative to their size. But interestingly, they only each have one vein, which means statistically a vascular problem is more likely to be venous than arterial.

Now when we look at the veins, we can see there's a big difference between the right adrenal and the left: so the left is drained into the renal vein and then into the vena cava; the right drains directly into the vena cava. And the reason for this is that in our body, our vena cava is a bit to the right of the midline, so it's closer to the right adrenal, so we have a very different venous drainage. Now this is medically extremely important, this difference, for two reasons. Firstly, if you have to remove an adrenal gland—an adrenalectomy—it is far more dangerous on the right side than the left side because there's a danger of damaging the vena cava, which is a catastrophic thing if it happens. So adrenal surgery is more difficult on the right side. It's also very relevant for something called adrenal venous sampling—I'll come back to that later—but for various reasons it's sometimes necessary to pass a catheter up to the vein to sample the hormones that the veins are producing, and that's very difficult to do on the right side because of this very narrow angle of the vein on the right side. It's relatively straightforward on the left side; it's very difficult on the right side. So this difference in anatomy between the right and left adrenal vein has real implications for medicine and surgery, and I'll come back to that later.

When we look more closely at the blood supply of the adrenal gland, we can see something else that's very typical for endocrine glands, which is something called a venous portal system. So I'll explain that in a second, but let's just look firstly at the anatomy. So if we look at the blood supply of the adrenal medulla, we see a dual blood supply. Firstly, we have arteries that pass straight down into the medulla and out. And this is important because the hormones that we're producing in the adrenal medulla—adrenaline, noradrenaline—these are emergency hormones. We need to get them out into the circulation quickly, so the medulla needs a fast blood supply. If the blood just trickles slowly through the organ, this is not going to work; we need a fast blood supply. But we can see there's a second blood supply: we can see there are blood vessels coming from the adrenal cortex to the medulla before they leave the circulation. And this is an example of a portal venous system.

And we have various examples of portal venous systems in our body, particularly in our endocrine glands; we have the portal vein itself. And what all of these do—it's an anatomical trick that the body uses to take a hormone being produced by one tissue to target it specifically to another tissue. And in this case, the hormones in the adrenal cortex act on the medulla. So the adrenal gland uses portal veins here—a portal venous system—to direct the hormone produced here to the target cells here. And this is much more efficient: if the hormone went out into the circulation, had to go round the whole body, whole circulation, and then come back to the medulla, this would be extremely inefficient. So this is a system whereby we can target hormones produced here to cells sitting here, and this is a trick—portal venous systems—this is a trick we're going to see in multiple endocrine organs. It's also the reason we have the portal vein to take blood from our intestine directly to our liver so we have this communication between the two.

Now I mentioned already that the hormones that we're producing here, adrenaline and noradrenaline, we need to get them out into the circulation very quickly. And you know that: so if you ever have a shock, you feel that adrenaline in your abdomen within one or two seconds; it's almost instantaneous. So how does adrenaline get into our blood so fast? Well, firstly we have an arterial supply, as we said. But something else we find very typically in the adrenal medulla are these very large veins called central veins or adrenomedullary veins. And you see here an example, and you can see these veins are very unusual, not only because they're very large, but the really unusual thing is they have a very muscular wall. Okay? Normally veins do not have much muscle in their wall, in contrast to arteries; they're not very contractile. But here we have large veins with a contractile wall. And what this does is it gives us the ability to close these veins or to open them. And when we open them, because they're very big veins, we then get a big rush of blood out into the system. So by having direct arterial supply and these very large veins that we can open or close, it gives us an anatomical mechanism to push adrenaline and noradrenaline very, very quickly into your system. And this is why if you get a shock, you feel adrenaline in your body almost instantaneously. Okay? It's all explained in the anatomy.

Okay, let's move on then to the histology. So if we look at the histology of the gland, firstly we can see we have these two parts: the cortex and the medulla, which we've already explained have very different origins. But if we look more closely at the cortex, we can see it has three different parts. So macroscopically it's just one structure, but microscopically with histology, we can see three different zones. And these are, going from outside in: a small zona glomerulosa, a large zona fasciculata, and then a small zona reticularis. And you can see each of these from the examples here can be distinguished histologically. And each of these names, as I'll show you, are simply describing what you see histologically.

But each of these different zones are producing different hormones. So we have three different hormones being produced in the adrenal cortex, and the cells in each of these zones are producing a different hormone. How do they do this? They are all lipid-hormone-producing cells. And remember from the last lecture, we said we make lipid hormones from cholesterol. So they're all storing cholesterol, they all have lots of cholesterol droplets within them, but they use that cholesterol to make different hormones. And this occurs because they express different enzymes. So the enzymes they express will determine the hormones that they can make, and you'll hear about that in biochemistry. But briefly, if they express an important enzyme called CYP17, then they can produce cortisol or the sex hormones. If they lack this enzyme, then they cannot produce those hormones. So if we look at the cells in the outer zone, the zona glomerulosa, they lack this enzyme here, so they cannot produce cortisol, they cannot produce the sex hormones, but instead they produce aldosterone, as we'll see. So what hormone these cells produce from this general precursor cholesterol depends on the enzymes that they express. And this is just to remind you: these are lipid-hormone-producing cells, so regardless of which hormone they produce, they're all lipid hormones, they all have this same machinery. So they all have the cholesterol droplets which they're going to use to make the hormones, they all have lots of smooth endoplasmic reticulum and tubular mitochondria which you need for lipid metabolism, and then as soon as they make the hormones, they are then released because we can't store lipid hormones.

Let's have a closer look at these zones. So we start on the outer part with the zona glomerulosa, and this is quite a small zone, just 10 to 15% of the cortex; nevertheless, functionally very important. We can see histologically that these cells are organized into clusters or balls; from this we get the name glomus, and from this we get the name zona glomerulosa. So the names are simply describing what you see histologically. We can see these cells in histology—you'll see this in histology class—they appear very white. Why? Because they're filled with cholesterol droplets; this gives them this white appearance. And as we said a couple of slides back, these cells lack this CYP17, this 17-alpha-hydroxylase—just go back here, they lack this enzyme here, so they can't produce these hormones here—so they go down this route, and what they finally produce is aldosterone.

And aldosterone is an incredibly important hormone: it regulates both the blood pressure and potassium in your body. So that relates to what we talked about in the last lecture: we said in the last lecture we have feedback mechanisms which control things like glucose and potassium in our blood. In the case of glucose it's insulin; in the case of potassium, it's the cells of the zona glomerulosa. They sense potassium in the blood and they respond: if we have too much potassium in the blood, they secrete aldosterone. Aldosterone works in the kidney; it promotes the secretion of potassium into the urine, therefore correcting the hyperkalemia. So this is an extremely important feedback mechanism to prevent hyperkalemia, which is extremely dangerous. The other stimulus for aldosterone is angiotensin II. So we have this renin-angiotensin system, extremely important for regulating blood pressure. When blood pressure is low, we activate the renin-angiotensin system, we produce angiotensin II, and angiotensin II stimulates the production of aldosterone. And aldosterone—another effect in the kidney in parallel to promoting excretion of potassium—it promotes the reabsorption of sodium. So it brings more sodium back into the blood, and this helps to correct our blood pressure. So aldosterone increases renal reabsorption of sodium, increases renal secretion of potassium, and this helps to maintain our blood pressure and maintain a normal potassium level. So it's an extremely important hormone, and there are diseases where this goes wrong, and I'll come back to that later.

The next zone is the zona fasciculata. This is the largest zone, makes up most of the cortex. Here we can see the cells are arranged in cords or fascicles, and from here we get the name zona fasciculata. These cells are also very rich in cholesterol granules, as you see from this diagram here, and sometimes they're called spongiocytes because they look like a sponge—they've got so many white dots of cholesterol granules, they look like a sponge. You can see both from the light microscopy and the electron microscopy that these cells are bathed in lots of blood, lots of sinusoids, again very typical for an endocrine gland, allowing the cells to sense what's in the blood and to secrete their hormone. And in this case, the hormone is cortisol. So cortisol is an extremely important hormone that has many different actions; it's widely used medically as well, and it's stimulated by something called ACTH, which is a hormone coming from our pituitary gland. And it has many different effects: it increases blood sugar, but from a medical perspective, it is an anti-inflammatory, it's an immune-suppressing drug, and it's very widely used for autoimmune diseases and to prevent rejection in transplantation. It's one of the most widely used drugs; it's an extremely important hormone.

And then we come to the final zone, the zona reticularis, also quite small. The cells here are organized in a sort of network, and from this we get the word rete, and from this we get the name zona reticularis. Again, you see lots of big vascular sinusoids between these cells, bathing these cells for reasons we've explained. Now a distinctive feature of these cells is they have lots of lysosomes and lots of granules here called lipofuscin. So lipofuscin is a breakdown product of lipid metabolism, and we see a lot of these in these cells. This is a very typical histological feature: you have lots of lipofuscin granules in the zona reticularis, again just reflecting the fact that these cells have a very high lipid metabolism. And the hormones we produce here are the sex hormones, the androgens. These are released into the blood and then they can be converted into testosterone or estrogen.

Okay, so those are the different zones of the cortex of the adrenal glands and what hormones they're producing and what they look like, their appearance. Let's move on to the medulla. So as we've already mentioned, the cells in the medulla are called chromaffin cells. This is because they stain with a chromate stain, so they have a histological name of chromaffin cells. And these sort of form clusters or connections of cells, again bathed by lots of blood. And as we mentioned right at the start, these are modified postganglionic sympathetic neurons. These are cells derived from the neural crest which migrated out, and instead of stopping in peripheral ganglia like all the other cells, they continued migrating, they invaded the developing adrenal cortex, and they formed the adrenal medulla. Okay? So these are neurons which have been converted into endocrine cells. So they're innervated by preganglionic fibers—so these are fibers that did not synapse in the peripheral ganglia, they continued all the way to these cells—and they're innervated here. And when we get the signal from the preganglionic fiber, we can then release the stored hormones.

So what are the hormones here? The main hormones are adrenaline and noradrenaline—or in American English, epinephrine and norepinephrine. These are extremely important substances, as you know; they are normally neurotransmitters in the sympathetic nervous system, but in this case, they're being released into the blood as hormones. Now these are derived from amino acids and they can be stored. So unlike the lipid hormones in the adrenal cortex, which we cannot store, these can be stored. They're stored in granules, so if we look at these cells in detail, we see they're full of granules, just like protein-hormone-producing cells, they're full of granules that have been produced and stored, and they're waiting for the signal. And when the signal comes, when we have a signal from the preganglionic sympathetic nerve fibers, we get a calcium rise within the cells, and all these stored granules are then immediately released into the circulation.

Now adrenaline and noradrenaline, they control what we call our fight and flight response. So from an evolutionary perspective, when we were in danger, when we were being chased by a dangerous animal, a predator, we needed to either run away or to fight back. And adrenaline and noradrenaline control this response: they increase our heart rate, they increase our circulation, they increase our blood sugar to our muscles; they allow us to either run away or to fight back, and this is our fight and flight response. And this is coming from the adrenal medulla, from these modified sympathetic neurons. And as we said, this is an emergency system; it has to act quickly. You don't have time to produce the hormones, you don't have time to wait for them to gradually trickle into the circulation, you need to have them immediately. So they're stored in granules, they are released as soon as you get the danger signal, and then because of those anatomical adaptations we talked about—these large adrenomedullary veins—we can push it directly into the circulation. And this is the reason why if you get a shock, you feel adrenaline in your body almost instantaneously. Okay? It's all explained in the anatomy.

Okay, so one way we can illustrate what these different parts of the adrenal gland are doing is by looking at diseases where they become over- or under-active. So let's start with adrenal insufficiency. This is often called Addison's disease, after Thomas Addison who described it. What happens if you lose the activity of these hormones in your adrenal cortex—if you lose aldosterone, if you lose cortisol—what are the effects? You can see that there are various different causes: worldwide, historically, things like TB were a major cause; these days it's mostly autoimmune. But because these hormones have many functions, if we lose these hormones it has many different symptoms, and this can be quite insidious, and the diagnosis of Addison's disease is often very delayed. Because if you look at these symptoms—things like fatigue, tiredness, lack of energy, etc.—these are all non-specific symptoms, these are symptoms that GPs are confronted with every day from many patients, so distinguishing which one has Addison's disease from all the others can be quite difficult and diagnosis is often delayed.

But there is one important clinical sign of Addison's disease, and this is something called hyperpigmentation. You see an example here: this is someone who's got a dark pigmentation of their mucous membranes, and this is an attempt from the body to stimulate the adrenal gland. I'll explain that on the next slide. But before we get there, just to help you remember this disease, there have been some famous examples of people with this disease. This is probably the most famous example—anybody recognize this man? Quick history lesson. [Student: John F. Kennedy.] John F. Kennedy, he was President of the United States. He famously had Addison's disease. He had to take steroid injections to replace the hormones that he was not producing in his adrenal gland. It's not what killed him, obviously, but he was a famous example of Addison's disease.

So why do these patients get this hyperpigmentation? They get hyperpigmentation because the body is trying to stimulate the adrenal gland. As we'll talk about when we talk about the hypothalamus and the pituitary gland, we have feedback systems in our endocrine system. So endocrine organs like the adrenal gland are under the control of the pituitary gland, which is under the control of the hypothalamus. Don't worry about that now, we'll go into much more detail in another lecture. But just for now, there are hormones coming from here which control the adrenal gland, in this case ACTH, and then there's feedback back to the brain. And when the adrenal gland is not producing cortisol, the brain registers this and starts producing much more ACTH in an attempt to stimulate the adrenal gland, and it's the ACTH that causes this pigmentation. Okay? So this is why in Addison's disease we get pigmentation: because our body starts producing massive amounts of ACTH because we've lost the feedback to try to correct the problem. And this is an important clinical sign of Addison's disease, which as I say is difficult to diagnose because the symptoms are often quite non-specific.

Here you see a couple of examples of Addison's disease: this is tuberculosis, this is an adrenal gland that has been destroyed by tuberculosis—these are called caseating granulomas, caseating because it looks like cheese, this is chronic inflammation destroying the adrenal glands. This is an example of septicemia destroying the adrenal glands. So there's different ways that you can destroy your adrenal glands, but the most common cause now in countries like Switzerland would be autoimmune disease.

Let's now flip this. We've looked at underactivity of the adrenal gland; what happens if we have overactivity? This produces something called Conn's syndrome, described by Jerome Conn in the 50s. And if we have an overproduction of the hormones of the adrenal gland, and particularly if we have an overproduction of aldosterone, this produces hypertension. So we said the aldosterone is normally defending our blood pressure; it's normally increasing sodium reabsorption in the kidney to increase blood sodium, increase blood pressure. If we have an overproduction of aldosterone, we present with hypertension. Now originally this was thought to be a relatively rare syndrome; actually, more recently it's become clear that it's probably a significant cause of all the hypertension that's out there.

Now the key question in this disease is whether it's being produced by a single adenoma, and that's an important question because if it is, if you remove that adenoma, you solve the problem, you cure the patient. So knowing whether they have an adenoma or not that's producing this Conn's syndrome is extremely important. Now you can do imaging, you can do CT or MRI, and you may see a lesion, but you don't know for sure that that is active: is that just an incidental finding, or is that lump on the CT scan, is that actually producing the aldosterone, is that causing the Conn's syndrome? And you need to know that before you subject the patient to the risk of surgery; you need to know that this adenoma is actually causing this syndrome and if you remove it, it's going to solve the problem. So this is why we need to do adrenal venous sampling, which I mentioned, where you pass a catheter up the vena cava into the adrenal vein, you sample what is being produced—they sample the amount of cortisol/aldosterone—and if you see a massive overproduction on the side where you see the adenoma, then the chances are that that is the cause and that then you would be justified in removing it. But the problem, as I alluded to earlier, is that catheterizing the left adrenal vein is relatively straightforward, but the right side is much harder because of this tight angle with the vena cava, it's much, much more difficult, and this sometimes fails. So this underlines how this difference between the right and left adrenal veins—the anatomical difference—is medically extremely important.

Here's another example of overactivity: this is Cushing's syndrome. This was described by Harvey Cushing; he was a neurosurgeon in America in the First World War, and he described this syndrome. This is what happens if you have an overactivity of the adrenal cortex, particularly producing cortisol. Now primary Cushing's syndrome is relatively rare, but secondary is very common. So secondary occurs when people are given steroids, when they're given corticosteroids. And historically, steroids were very widely used for treating autoimmune disease and for preventing rejection in transplantation. So in the early days of transplantation, people received very high doses of steroids—today we have other drugs that they can have, but in the earlier times they received very high amounts of steroids—and this can lead to Cushing's syndrome. And you see someone here with Cushing's syndrome: you see they get central obesity, they lose muscle, they develop diabetes, and other features you see here. This is what happens if you have too much cortisol; this is the Cushing's syndrome.

So here you see some examples of adrenal glands: so normal glands here; underactive, Addison's disease; overactive would be Conn's or Cushing's syndrome.

And then lastly, we can also have overactivity of the adrenal medulla. We can have tumors of the adrenal medulla that cause an overproduction of hormones there, and this is called a pheochromocytoma. Okay, so if we have a patient that we've decided we're going to remove the adrenal gland—we've done our venous sampling, we're sure they have an adenoma, we're sure that it's functional, we're sure that we want to remove it—how do we do it?
