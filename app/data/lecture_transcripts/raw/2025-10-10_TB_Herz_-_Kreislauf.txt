Emotions are a tricky subject, both in life and in psychology. Questions like "Why do you make me feel this way?" are not only confusing on a personal level, but also on a scientific level.

First, let's distinguish emotions from feelings and moods. An emotion is an immediate, specific positive or negative response to internal thoughts or external environmental events. A feeling is the subjective experience of an emotion. It's what we refer to when we say things like "I feel angry" or "I feel happy." A mood, meanwhile, is a diffuse, longer-lasting emotional state that doesn't necessarily have an identifiable object or trigger. So if something frustrating happens to you, you might immediately feel the emotional response of anger, and for the next few days you might be in a bad mood.

Let's move on to the various components of an emotion. So we know that emotions are immediate and specific responses. These responses have at least three parts: physiological, cognitive, and behavioral. A physiological component of an emotion might be bodily arousal, like a racing heart or tense muscles. The cognitive component of an emotion is the subjective conscious experience of emotion. And the behavioral component of an emotion is the characteristic overt expressions of the emotion, behaviors that other people can read, like particular facial expressions or posture.

Charles Darwin studied this third component of emotions in his 1872 book *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals*, in which he argued that particular emotional responses tend to accompany the same emotional states in all members of a species. So according to Darwin's argument, all of the members of a species will display the same behavior when they feel a particular emotion. All cats will display the same behavior when they are happy, and all humans will have the same facial expression and behavior when scared. With this evidence, Darwin suggested that the expression of emotion is genetically determined. This behavior has evolved and has been passed down through generations in our genes.

Now let's look at some theories of how emotions work.

Let's start with the common-sense view. In this view, a stimulus prompts an emotion, which in turn prompts some bodily arousal. So in this case, a frightening spectacle fills our villain with fear, which makes him sweat and makes his heart rate rise.

In the mid-1880s, two scholars, William James and Carl Lange, independently came up with similar theories that contradicted this common-sense view. James and Lange both suggested that instead of emotions causing bodily arousal, bodily arousal causes emotions. So to return to our previous example, that would mean that the sight of the hero on his threshold makes our villain's heart beat faster, his hands tremble, and his palms sweat, and these physiological changes cause him to feel fear.

But this theory is vulnerable to a few criticisms. First, it is possible to have physiological arousal without any accompanied emotion. For instance, your palms might sweat because it's hot, but that sweat won't make you feel nervous, just kind of gross. Second, physiological changes can be too slow to precede the conscious experience of emotion. Our villain might feel scared as soon as he sees our hero, but it might take a few seconds for him to start sweating. Third, the same set of physiological responses are associated with very different emotional experiences. A faster heart rate could mean fear, but it could also mean anger, or surprise, or joy. Finally, there is evidence that physiological feedback is not necessary for emotion. For example, patients with spinal cord injuries who have lost most of their physiological feedback still experience the same emotions as before. However, in the James-Lange theory's favor, there is notably some slight evidence of dissipation in their emotional reactions.

As an alternative to the James-Lange theory, two other theorists, Walter Cannon and Philip Bard, proposed that the physiological arousal of emotions and the experience of emotions are entirely separate processes. That is, a stimulus causes the emotion and physiological arousal independently. Neither one causes the other. So the sight of our hero causes our villain both to feel fear and for his palms to sweat and heart to race. But contrary to this theory, there have been many reports of physiological stimuli influencing emotions. So it's unlikely that physiology and emotions are completely disconnected.

One suggestion of how arousal and the experience of emotion may be connected, but not causal, was proposed in the 1960s by Stanley Schachter and Jerome Singer as the Schachter-Singer two-factor theory, which introduces the factor of cognition. The theory suggests that when a stimulus elicits some arousal, this arousal elicits some cognition, which interprets the source of the arousal, and then this interpretation then elicits the experience of emotion. From the stimulus, it takes two different factors—the arousal and the cognition—to elicit the emotion. Hence the name Schachter-Singer two-factor theory.

For example, say that you feel your face start to flush. According to this theory, you'll automatically use cues from your environment to help explain this flush. You might be mid-workout, or someone might have just confessed their love to you. Although the arousal is the same, the cognitive evaluation of the stimulus is very different across these two scenarios. And so the emotions you would feel would also be very different: say pride versus shyness, for instance. To return to our previous example, if the sight of our hero makes our villain's heart beat faster, then their cognitive evaluation of the situation—say remembering that the hero is a secret service agent here to save the world, or that he is their long-lost love from primary school—will determine their emotional response to the situation. Again, with this theory, it's about the interpretation of the stimuli and the arousal, which leads to the emotion.

So let's just quickly review these four theories of emotion to help you remember them.
First, the common-sense view is that a stimulus will cause an emotion, which causes arousal.
Second, the James-Lange theory reverses emotion and arousal, suggesting instead that a stimulus causes arousal, which then causes emotion.
Third, the Cannon-Bard theory separates out emotion and arousal as independent reactions to the stimulus.
And finally, the Schachter-Singer two-factor theory adds another factor to the cause of emotion: after a stimulus elicits some arousal, you cognitively assess the stimulus, and that assessment causes the emotion.

Zuerst einmal: Was sind Schubspannungen? Wir können Schubspannungen in einem Festkörper definieren, das ist dann relativ einfach: Eine Schubspannung $\tau$ ist die tangential wirkende Scherkraft pro Flächeneinheit – also eigentlich das Gegenstück zum Druck: Der Druck wirkt senkrecht, die Schubspannung wirkt parallel. Wenn wir also einen Festkörper haben mit einer Kontaktfläche $A$ und es wirkt eine Scherkraft $F$, dann wird hier dieser Körper geschert, und die Schubspannung ist dann einfach definiert als $\tau = F / A$, parallel wirkende Kraft über die Kontaktfläche.

In einer Flüssigkeit ist es ein bisschen komplizierter, dort ist die Schubspannung eine Funktion des Geschwindigkeitsgradienten. Das schauen wir uns hier kurz an: Wir haben die Gefäßwand der Arteriole zum Beispiel, das Endothel, welches die Arteriole auskleidet, und hier im Blut eine Geschwindigkeitsverteilung. Die Geschwindigkeit ist in der Mitte des Gefäßes am höchsten und nimmt dann Richtung Endothel immer weiter ab, bis es auf dem Endothel gleich null ist. Der Gradient hier, der wichtige, ist der vom Endothel in die Mitte des Gefäßes, also $du / dz$. Und wenn wir von einer newtonschen Flüssigkeit ausgehen – wir können das Blut annähernd als newtonsche Flüssigkeit anschauen –, dann gilt, dass hier die Schubspannung gleich $\mu$, das ist die Viskosität des Blutes, mal eben dieser Geschwindigkeitsgradient ist ($\tau = \mu \cdot \frac{du}{dz}$).

Und wenn diese Geschwindigkeit auf dem Endothel immer gleich null ist, dann erkennen wir auch sofort, dass wenn die Geschwindigkeit hier in der Mitte sich erhöht oder die Durchflussrate erhöht, dann erhöht sich auch die Schubspannung, weil sich dieser Gradient auch erhöht hat. Oder anders gesagt: Bei einem gegebenen Gefäßdurchmesser erhöht sich die Schubspannung, wenn der Blutfluss erhöht wird. Diese Schubspannung erhöht sich auch, wenn zum Beispiel die Blutviskosität erhöht wird. Sowohl Erhöhung des Flusses und damit der Geschwindigkeit wie auch der Blutviskosität erhöhen die Schubspannung.

In gewissen Arteriolen und Arterien reagiert nun das Endothel auf eine Veränderung der Schubspannung, indem es die Produktion von NO, von Stickstoffmonoxid, moduliert. Wenn die Schubspannung steigt, dann wird mehr von diesem Stickstoffmonoxid produziert. Das NO, Stickstoffmonoxid, wird aber sehr schnell innerhalb von Sekunden wieder abgebaut, und zwar indem es an Hämoglobin gebunden wird.
